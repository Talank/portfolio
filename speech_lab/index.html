What is actually being measured, and what is not

Measured from your waveform, locally: how many syllables you produced, which one carried the stress (from length, loudness and pitch together), how uneven the syllable lengths were (nPVI — the standard number for telling a stress-timed rhythm from a syllable-timed one), your speaking rate, and whether the final consonant was released.

Measured by a recogniser: whether an American English speech recogniser heard the word you were aiming at, and what it heard instead. What it heard instead is the useful part — “sink” and “tink” are different failures of the same sound and want different corrections.

How the colours are placed: a letter turns red because the recogniser's transcript, aligned to the spelling, lost or changed the sound there — and the red widens to the whole digraph, so “th” colours together. A beat turns red because its measured loudness, length and pitch missed the profile English wants for that syllable. When neither source can be lined up, the row says so instead of colouring a guess.

Not measured: per-phoneme accuracy scores. Tools like ELSA produce those with a purpose-trained neural model running on their servers. There is no honest way to compute one in a page like this, so this app does not print a number it cannot stand behind — and the colour bands here are placed by position, never by an invented per-sound percentage.

Where your voice goes: the acoustic analysis happens entirely in this tab and the audio is never uploaded or stored. The recogniser is the exception — Chrome implements the Web Speech API by sending the utterance to Google. If that is not acceptable, the acoustic checks still work with the recogniser disabled.

The reference voice is your browser's speech synthesiser, not a recording of a person. It models the consonants and vowels well and the rhythm only roughly.

Type or speak a sentence. The rules here are not a general grammar checker — they look only for the error classes that come out of a Nepali first language, because those are systematic and therefore worth naming.
