# 10 — The Tortoise and the Hare: Fast and Slow Pointers

On the tenth island there is a road that goes in a circle. But the crew does not know that the road is a circle. As they walked, the view began to feel like the same view again and again.

Chopper said — [chopper:afraid]"I feel like we are going round and round. But how would we know for certain?"[/]

Robin smiled and said — [robin]"Let us send two people. Let one walk slowly, and let the other run twice as fast."[/]

@algo kachhuwa
The rule of the tortoise and the hare is this. Put both of them at the start of the road. At every step move the tortoise one step, and move the hare two steps. If the hare reaches the end, the road is straight, and there is no cycle in it. If the hare and the tortoise meet at the same place, the road is a circle, and there is a cycle in it. If you need to find the mouth of the cycle, then after the meeting leave one finger where it is and send the other one back to the start. Now walk both of them one step at a time together, and wherever they meet is the mouth of the cycle. No notebook is needed for this, so the space is O(1).
@end

There is magic in it. If the road is straight the fast one reaches the end first, and they never meet. But if the road is a circle both of them start going round inside that circle. And the fast one closes on the slow one by one step on every lap, so sooner or later they meet exactly.

Think for a moment about how beautiful that is. If they meet, the road is a circle. If they do not meet, the road is straight. No notebook was needed, and nothing had to be written down.

Here is another use of these same two fingers, which is even simpler. You have to find which node is exactly in the middle of a linked list. Run the same move again, and move one of them one step and the other two steps. When the fast one reaches the end, the slow one is exactly in the middle. You never had to count the length, and it finished in a single pass.

Now a deeper question arrives, which confuses a lot of people. We know there is a cycle, but where does the cycle begin?

The answer to it is strange, and it sounds like magic. It is right there in the last part of the rule. There is mathematics behind why it works, and tonight you do not have to do that arithmetic. Just remember this much — after the meeting, send one of them home, and then walk together.

@read hush
The reason this works is a small one. Inside a loop the hare comes one pace closer to the tortoise on every step, so the distance falls one at a time until it reaches zero. The distance can never jump over, and that is the proof that they must meet.

[nami]"It does."[/] Nami said. [nami]"Think of the array as a linked list, and think of every index as pointing towards the value inside it. Once a number is repeated, two indexes point at the same place, and a cycle forms there."[/]

[robin]"And the mouth of the cycle is the repeated number."[/] Robin said.

That sounds strange when you hear it, but that is all it is. Being able to see an array as a linked list is the real lesson here.

Chopper is tired. He lay down slowly in the grass. The secret of the circular road is out, and now it is possible to sleep.

@tier medium

The grass was warm. Chopper had not fallen fully asleep yet. Robin said quietly — [robin]"This two-speed move is useful in two or three more places."[/]

In the first place you have to remove the back half of a linked list from exactly the middle, or add something in the middle. You do not have to count the length to find the middle, because two speeds are enough. It is a small saving, but it finishes the work in a single pass.

In the second place there is the game of happy numbers. Take some number, add the squares of its digits, and keep repeating the same work. Sometimes you arrive at one. Sometimes you end up stuck going round in a circle.

@read teach
There is no linked list here at all. But every number points at another number, and that is what a [..]linked list[..] is. Move the slow one one step and the fast one two steps. If you arrive at one, the number is happy, and if they meet, the number is unhappy.

In the third place comes the question of how long the cycle is. After the meeting, keep one finger where it is, and take the other one around one lap, counting the steps. When it comes back to that same place, the count is the length of the cycle.

Robin added — [robin]"The heart of this move is not the linked list. The heart of it is that wherever there is a 'points at the next one', a cycle is possible, and a cycle is found by two speeds."[/]

Robin watched the two runners and said — [robin]"The tortoise and the hare are three lines in Python. One of those lines is doing something clever that most people never notice."[/]

The clever line is the one that keeps the loop alive. The fast runner takes two steps, so before it moves you must know both that it is standing somewhere and that there is somewhere after it. Two questions, joined by the word "and".

@read teach
Here is the part worth staying awake for. Python reads that pair from the left, and the moment the first half is false it stops and never looks at the second. So asking first whether the fast one exists, and only then whether the one after it exists, is safe. Turn the two around and the program crashes at the end of every chain, because you asked what comes after nobody. The order of those two questions is not a matter of taste. It is the guard rail.

[chopper]"So the word 'and' is doing the checking?"[/] Chopper asked.

[robin]"The word 'and' is refusing to do the checking."[/] Robin said. [robin]"That is what makes it safe. It is a door that closes before the dangerous question is ever asked."[/]

And there is a second matter, which is what sameness means here. When the two runners meet, you are asking whether they are standing on the same thing — not whether the two things look alike. Python keeps those two questions apart, and gives them different words. One asks whether these are the very same object. The other asks whether they carry equal values.

[nami]"And on a chain, two different links can easily carry the same number."[/] Nami said. [nami]"If you ask the looks-alike question you will announce a circle that is not there."[/]

So on this island you ask the same-thing question, always. It is also cheaper, because it compares where they stand rather than what they hold.

And the last small note. A chain that has ended is written as nothing, and nothing counts as false. So the whole safety check reads almost as plain speech: while the fast one is there, and the one after it is there. The code says the sentence you would have said out loud.

The road was still a circle, but now it was not frightening. Once you know about it, even a circular road feels like home.

Robin said — [robin]"Let me ask a question. Why are the two speeds exactly one and two? Why not three and five?"[/]

Chopper shook his head. Robin answered it herself — [robin]"Because if the difference is one, then inside the cycle the fast one closes on the slow one by exactly one step every lap. If it closes by one step at a time, it can never jump over. If the difference is two, both of them can jump and step over each other at the same moment."[/]

She added — [robin]"So one and two is safe. It is a small thing, but it is what gives the move its proof."[/]

Now let us also walk slowly through that magical reasoning for finding the mouth of the cycle. This one is fine to drift through, because you do not have to do the arithmetic and hearing the shape is enough.

Suppose there are A steps from the start to the mouth of the cycle. There are B steps from the mouth to the meeting place. And there are C steps from the meeting place back round to the mouth. So the length of the cycle is B plus C.

The slow one has walked A plus B steps. The fast one has walked exactly double, and it has gone round the cycle some number of times to arrive at that same place. Putting the two accounts together gives one beautiful result, and it is that A equals C, with whole laps of the cycle added on.

Now, what does that mean? The distance from the meeting place to the mouth is equal to the distance from the start to the mouth. So sending one of them home and walking both one step at a time makes them meet exactly at the mouth.

Then there is one more use, and it is an easy one, and that is counting from the end of a linked list. We already met the move of sending one finger K steps ahead on the last island. That one is in this same family, because there too there are two fingers, the start is different, and the walking is together.

The only difference is that there the speed was the same and the distance was different, and here the distance is the same and the speed is different. Both moves are two faces of one idea.

That brings us to another problem, and it needs tools from both tonight's island and last night's.

The linked list has to be put into this order — the first node, then the last node, then the second node, then the one before last, then the third. You come inwards from the two ends by turns.

Robin said — [robin]"A linked list will not let you read backwards. So turn the back half around."[/]

The work breaks into three steps, and you already have all three.

In the first step, send the tortoise and the hare and find the middle. That is tonight's second move.

In the second step, reverse the half that runs from the middle to the end. That is the ninth island's dance of the three fingers.

In the third step, join the two halves by turns — one from the front, one from the reversed half, one from the front again.

The time is O(n) and it needs no new room. Watch one thing. After you cut at the middle, make the tail of the first half empty, or the two halves stay joined together and the loop never stops.

Chopper said in his sleep — [chopper]"Three easy things joined up and made a hard thing."[/] Robin nodded. Hard problems are usually like that, and there is no new move in them — the old moves have simply been set down in order.

Chopper was nearly gone. Robin said quietly — [robin]"Let us walk the repeated-number problem from earlier once more, slowly, because tonight's whole move is hiding in it. There is no chain here, and no road either. There is only an array."[/]

There are n plus one numbers, and every one of them lies between one and n. So at least one number must appear twice. Find it, without changing the array, and without a notebook.

[nami]"With a notebook this is nothing."[/] Nami said. [nami]"Write down what you have seen. The first one you meet twice is the answer."[/]

[robin]"That is O(n) room, and tonight's condition will not give it."[/] Robin said. [robin]"So look at the array with a different eye. Stand at a place, read the number written there, and walk to the place with that number. Read again, and walk again."[/]

[usopp]"That is just a road."[/] Usopp said, sitting up.

[robin]"It is a road. And because some number appears twice, two different places send you to the same place. A road where two arrows arrive at one point has to fold back on itself."[/] Robin said. [robin]"So there is a cycle in this road, and the mouth of the cycle is the repeated number."[/]

After that it is tonight's move exactly as it stands. One walker moves a step at a time and the other two at a time until they meet. Then one goes back to the start, both move one step at a time, and where they meet again is the mouth.

The time is O(n), the room is O(1), and the array is never written to. Half asleep, Chopper said — [chopper]"It was a road the whole time, and nobody had drawn it."[/]

Robin and Nami were still awake. Nami drew a ring of stones in the dust, and wrote a number on each. [nami]"Each number says how far to jump, forward if it is positive and backward if it is negative."[/] Nami said. [nami]"The ring wraps around. Is there a cycle?"[/]

[robin]"It is the duplicate number again, a list of numbers that is secretly a road."[/] Robin said. [robin]"Only now the road can go both ways, and the question has two conditions."[/]

A real cycle must move in one direction only, all forward or all backward. And it must be longer than one stone. A stone that jumps back onto itself does not count.

@read teach
So start the tortoise and the hare from a stone. On every step, check the direction. If the next jump turns around, or lands where it stands, this road has no cycle. If the two runners meet, a cycle exists. When a road dies, mark every stone on it as dead, so that nobody has to walk it twice.

[nami]"Why do the marks matter?"[/] Nami asked. [robin]"Because a dead road stays dead, whoever walks it."[/] Robin said. [robin]"The marks keep the whole search O(n). Without them, every start would walk the same dead roads again."[/]

For two, minus one, one, two, two, there is a cycle through three stones, all moving forward. For minus two, one, minus one, minus two, minus two, there is none, because the only circle turns around halfway.

Chopper really has fallen asleep now. Dew had started coming down on the grass. The circular road was still there, and it was turning quietly.

Robin drew a circle in the dust of the road with her finger. She said — [robin]"People do not know they are going round in circles, because the view keeps changing. But the view changing and moving forward are not the same thing."[/]

That is tonight's lesson, and it is a little hard. Whether you are going round in a circle or not is something you cannot learn by walking at one speed. To find out, somebody has to walk at a different speed.

So when some piece of work feels like it is moving forward but is not, change the speed. Go faster, or go slower. A different speed shows a different thing, and if they meet, the truth comes out.

Next comes the second lesson, which is the most beautiful thing tonight. We turned a problem about numbers into a problem about a road. Being able to change the form of a problem is the highest floor of the skill. When the answer does not come, try changing the shape of the question.

Chopper's breathing was slowing down. The dew was settling on the grass, and the circular road was not frightening any more.
