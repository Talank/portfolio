# 21 — The Power of Memory: Dynamic Programming, Part Two

On the twenty-first island it is the same house, but today the wall is larger. The papers are stuck up in two directions, making rows and columns.

Robin said — [robin]"In yesterday's problems only one thing changed. In today's problems two things change. So today the wall becomes a table."[/]

The first problem is the coins. There are unlimited numbers of coins of different values. What is the smallest number of coins needed to make some amount?

Luffy said — [luffy:excited]"That is easy. Start from the largest coin and it is done."[/] Shaking her head, Robin said — [robin:firm]"That is the greedy way, and it goes wrong here."[/]

Think about it this way. The coins are one, three and four, and you have to make six. The greedy way takes four, then one, then one again, and that is three coins. But if you take three and three you get there in two coins. That is how greed failed.

@algo talika
The rule for memory in two dimensions is this. First write down your case, that is, work out which numbers together name one sub-question completely. Those numbers become the rows and columns of the table. Write the answer to the smallest case first, and that is the base. Now fill the squares of the table in order, and in every square try each of the choices you have, one at a time. Every choice takes you to a smaller square that has already been filled, so read the answer there directly and add your own cost. Write the best answer among all the choices into that square. Whatever is written in the last square is the answer to the whole question.
@end

[nami]"In the coin problem that same rule applies like this."[/] Nami said. [nami]"Making zero takes zero coins, and that is the base."[/]

[chopper]"And after that?"[/] Chopper asked.

[nami]"Climb from one up to the target, and at every amount try every coin."[/] Nami said. [nami]"If I take this coin, the answer for the remaining amount is already on the wall, and you add one to it."[/]

[robin]"Whichever comes out smallest across all the coins is the answer for this amount."[/] Robin said.

The second problem is the longest rising run, and it is called [..]LIS[..]. You have to pick some numbers out of the array, without breaking their order, and make the longest rising sequence.

The simple way is this. For every place, ask — [aside]"How long is the longest sequence that ends here?"[/] To work that out, look backwards. Anybody smaller than me lets me add one to their answer. Take the largest of them all, and this is O(n²).

There is a faster way as well, which uses binary search and reaches O(n log n). Tonight you do not need those details. It is enough to know that sometimes, when the tools of two islands are joined, a new speed comes out.

@read hush
Here is the easy way to check the answer once the table is full. Hold the smallest case in the table against the answer you counted by hand, then do the same for the second smallest. If the base is right then every box above it is right on its own, because each box is built out of the boxes below it.

@read teach
The third problem is the bag, and it is called the [..]Knapsack[..]. The bag carries a limited weight. Every item has a weight and a value. So what is the most value you can carry?

Here two things change, and they are how many items I have looked at and how much room is left in the bag. So the table is two-dimensional.

The same question is asked in every square. If you take this item, add its value, and look at the earlier answer with the remaining room reduced. If you do not take it, the answer from the previous row stays as it was. Take the larger of the two.

The fourth problem is the distance between two words. What is the smallest number of changes needed to turn one word into the other, where you may add, remove, or replace?

Again a two-dimensional table is built, holding how many letters of the first word and how many of the second have been looked at. If the letters match, take the answer from the corner diagonally back, unchanged. If they do not match, take the smallest of the three neighbours and add one.

Now remember lightly how to recognise DP. If the question asks [aside]"the most"[/], [aside]"the fewest"[/], or [aside]"in how many ways"[/], and there is a choice at every step, and the choices are joined to one another, then it is DP.

The papers on the wall are quiet. Outside, the wind is moving. The table is filling itself in, one square at a time.

@read hush
The order in which you fill the table is itself a rule. Before you fill any box, every box it looks at must already be filled. Get the order backwards and the code still runs and still gives an answer, but that answer is built out of empty boxes.

The table was half full. Robin shook the chalk dust off her hands and said — [robin]"There are a few squares left on this wall."[/]

The first square is the longest run shared by two words. You have to pick some letters out of both, without breaking their order, and make the same sequence.

The table is two-dimensional. If the letters match, add one to the answer at the corner diagonally back. If they do not match, take the larger of the one above and the one to the left. That is all of it.

This problem is worth remembering, because its shape comes back in many places. From the tool that shows the difference between two files to the matching of sequences in biology, this same shape turns up.

In the second square is the question of whether an amount can be made, where every number may be used only once. This is called the subset total.

Every square of the table says whether, having looked at this many items, this amount can be made or not. Here the answer is only yes or no. It can be done by taking this item, or by not taking it, and it is enough for either one of the two to be true.

In the third square is the question of whether an array can be divided into two parts with equal totals. This is the same subset total, but it has arrived in new clothes. If the grand total is odd, say no immediately. If it is even, ask whether half of it can be made.

Robin said — [robin]"That is the true way to learn DP. When a new question arrives, ask which question you already know this is the clothing of."[/]

Robin drew the row of squares and said — [robin]"Building the table from the bottom is where Python is at its shortest. It is also where it keeps the single worst trap in this language. Both live in the same line."[/]

For one row of squares there is nothing to it. A zero repeated as many times as you need, and the row exists. No loop, no filling, one line. Then you walk it and each square asks the squares behind it.

@read teach
The square of squares is where the trap lives. You might think you can make one row and then repeat that row as many times as you need. You cannot. What you get is not many rows — it is one row with many names looking at it. Write into what you believe is the third row and the same value appears in every row at once, because there was only ever one. The program does not complain. It gives you an answer that is confidently, quietly wrong.

[luffy]"They are all the same shelf."[/] Luffy said.

[nami]"You did not build ten shelves."[/] Nami said. [nami]"You hung ten labels on one shelf. And this is the third night we have met this — the notebook that will not take a changeable heading, the maze that saved the same path over and over. A name is not a thing."[/]

The right way is to say it once for each row, so that each time round a fresh row is genuinely made. It is barely longer to write and it is the only version that is true.

[chopper]"How would I catch it if I did it wrong?"[/] Chopper asked.

[robin]"By the shape of the wrongness."[/] Robin said. [robin]"A whole row identical to another row, when the numbers had no reason to agree — that is always this and never anything else. It is worth recognising by sight, because the day you meet it you will otherwise spend an hour blaming the arithmetic."[/]

And there is a last move, which is where the table gets small. Very often a square only ever looks at the row directly above it. If that is true, you do not need the whole square at all. Keep two rows, the one above and the one you are filling. When the row is done, let them trade places — which in this language is a single line with no spare name, exactly as it was on the first night.

[nami]"The cost goes from the whole grid down to one row."[/] Nami said. [nami]"For a table of a thousand by a thousand, that is a million squares becoming a thousand. And often it is the difference between fitting in memory and not."[/]

Outside, the wind was still moving. The table on the wall was now almost completely full.

Robin said — [robin]"Now let us say that fast way of doing the longest rising run, because it really is sweet to hear."[/]

Keep an empty array. For every number, look for its place inside that array, that is, look for the first place that is not smaller than it, and that is found by binary search.

Put yourself in that place. If you reach the end of the array, the length goes up by one.

There is an honest thing here. That array is not the real sequence. It is only the memory of [aside]"to make a sequence of this length, what is the smallest number that can be at the end"[/]. But the length comes out right, and that was what was wanted.

There are the other shapes of the bag problem. If the items are unlimited, the table is one-dimensional, and the walk goes in the forward direction.

If every item may be taken only once, the walk goes in the reverse direction, or the same item gets counted again. That one difference makes two different worlds.

And what do you do if the items are limited in number? That can be broken up in powers of two and turned into the take-only-once problem. It is a clever move, and tonight you do not need its details.

Then comes a different family, and that is DP that divides into pieces. For example, the question of which order to join the pearls in to make a necklace most cheaply is in this family.

Here the case is two indexes, that is, from where to where. And inside there is a loop that tries every place to cut in the middle.

So its time reaches O(n³). It sounds expensive, but in this family that is the natural cost, and here n is usually small.

The best way of bracketing falls in this same family. So does the cheapest order of multiplying. So does the question of how few cuts are needed to make palindromes.

Here is something practical. What do you do if you need not only the answer but also the road? For example, you need not only how long the longest shared run is, but what it actually is.

Once the table is filled, walk it backwards. Ask at every square — [aside]"Where did this answer come from?"[/] Follow that road, noting things down as you go. Turn what you noted around at the end.

Then comes one last idea, which is the deepest one. For DP to work, two qualities are needed.

The first quality is that the best answer to the large problem has to be built out of the best answers to the small problems. The second quality is that the same small problem has to come back many times, or there is no benefit in remembering it.

Where those two are missing, trying to apply DP is wasted effort. And where those two are present, DP looks almost like magic.

That brings us to something beautiful, and it makes two of tonight's questions into one.

The question is this. What is the longest subsequence inside a string that reads the same backwards? You may skip letters, but you may not change their order.

Luffy said — [luffy]"That is a new problem."[/] Robin shook her head and said — [robin]"It is not. It is that same longest common subsequence, and you already have the second string."[/]

The move is one sentence long. Reverse that same string, and take the longest common subsequence of the original and the reversed one. That is the answer.

Why it is true becomes plain when you hear it. The part that stays the same read backwards is the part that is found in the same order whether you read from the front or from the back. Reading from the back is reading the reversed string from the front. So the longest part common to both is exactly the answer you were looking for.

And now another answer comes out of it for free. How few letters must be added to a string to make it a palindrome? As many letters as did not get into that long subsequence. Which is to say, subtract that answer from the length of the string.

The time is O(n²), and the room is O(n²) as well, though remembering two rows at a time is enough.

Robin had not picked the chalk back up. She said — [robin]"One more, and it is tonight's table with one word changed. You asked how much two words have in common. Now ask how far apart they are."[/]

Two words are given, and three moves are allowed — put a letter in, take a letter out, or change one letter into another. What is the fewest moves that turn the first word into the second?

[chopper]"That sounds like a completely different question."[/] Chopper said.

[nami]"It is the same table."[/] Nami said. [nami]"Rows for one word and columns for the other. In each square, the fewest moves that turn the first so-many letters into the second so-many."[/]

The rule inside a square has two cases. If the two letters standing at that row and column are the same, nothing has to be done at all, and the answer is the square up and to the left, unchanged. If they differ, it costs one move, and you take the best of three neighbours.

[usopp]"Why three?"[/] Usopp asked.

[robin]"Because there are three moves."[/] Robin said. [robin]"Up-and-left is changing a letter. Left is putting one in. Up is taking one out. The table is not a trick. Every arrow in it is one of the moves you were allowed in the first place."[/]

The edges matter as much as the middle. Turning any word into nothing costs one move for each letter, so the first row and the first column count straight up from nought.

The time is the two lengths multiplied, and so is the room, though two rows at a time are enough if you only want the number. Putting the chalk down, Robin said — [robin]"Every square in it is a small question, and the whole wall is only that same question asked in every size."[/]

The wall was full, and Robin left it full. She said — [robin]"There is a last one, and it does not go on the wall at all. It is the problem where the table turns out not to be needed."[/]

A row of stones is laid out, and a number is painted on each. Some of those numbers are less than nothing. Two players take turns, and Luffy goes first.

A turn works like this. Take some stones off the left end, and you must take at least two of them. Add up what you took, and that total goes into your own score. Then put one new stone back on the left end, with that same total painted on it. The row gets shorter every turn, and when a single stone is left the game stops.

[usopp]"So who wins?"[/] Usopp asked.

[robin]"Nobody wins."[/] Robin said. [robin]"Luffy is trying to make the gap between his score and yours as wide as he can. You are trying to make it as narrow as you can. The answer is that gap, on the day neither of you makes a mistake."[/]

[chopper]"Then Luffy should take the whole row on his first turn."[/] Chopper said.

[robin]"On some rows that is exactly right."[/] Robin said. [robin]"If every stone is worth having, take the lot and there is nothing left to decide. It is only a game because some stones are worth less than nothing."[/]

Here is the small thing worth staying awake for. Lay down three stones, and paint minus four on every one of them. There is not a good stone anywhere in that row. Luffy takes the first two, and that costs him eight. What is left is a stone of minus eight and a stone of minus four. Usopp has two stones and no choice at all. He takes both, and that costs him twelve. So Luffy ends four ahead, on a row made of nothing but debts.

He was never collecting treasure. He was collecting the gap. So he swallows the smaller poison himself, and leaves the larger one standing where somebody else has to pick it up.

[robin]"Now write down the case."[/] Robin said. [robin]"That is the sentence this whole island has been about. Which things together name one sub-question completely?"[/]

[chopper]"The whole row."[/] Chopper said. [chopper]"It could be in any shape at all."[/]

[robin]"Look at it after two moves, then say that again."[/] Robin said.

@read teach
It cannot be in any shape at all. Whatever has happened, the right-hand part of the row has never been touched. It is still exactly the stones you began with. And in front of it stands one stone, and that stone is always the total of everything eaten so far. It has to be. Adding does not care what order you added in, so however the pieces were merged, the front stone comes out the same. The case is not a row. The case is one number, and that number is how far along you have got.

[nami]"That is the seventh island."[/] Nami said. [nami]"The road with the stones, and the running total painted on each one. It never left. Tonight it turns out to be the whole state of the game."[/]

Now say the move again in that smaller language. Taking some stones off the front is the same as jumping forward to a later place. And your score is not something you have to add up. Your score is the running total at the place where you land. That is the entire game. Choose a place further along, take the running total there, and hand the row over.

[usopp]"Do I keep two scores, then, and a note of whose turn it is?"[/] Usopp asked.

[robin]"You keep one number."[/] Robin said. [robin]"How far ahead the player who is about to move can get. Whatever the other one manages from where you leave them comes straight off you. Your total, minus their best. The turn flag disappears, because the minus sign is the turn."[/]

The last saving is the sweetest one. Standing at some place, the jumps you may take are all the places further along. Stand one place later and it is that same list, with the nearest jump gone from it. So the two differ by a single entry.

Which means no loop inside a loop, and no table either. At every place there are only two things to weigh. Take the jump to the very next place, or decline it and settle for whatever the next place was already worth. Keep the better of the two, and walk backwards down the row.

[nami]"Earlier tonight two rows became one row."[/] Nami said. [nami]"Here one row becomes one number. Memory does not get smaller than that."[/]

There is one place to be careful, and it is where the walk ends. The opening move is exactly the set of jumps available from place one, so place one is where the answer sits. Walk down as far as one and stop there. Let the walk run one step further and you have quietly allowed somebody to take a single stone. The rules never permitted that, and about one row in eight comes back with a different answer.

There is a shorter version that keeps no second array at all. Instead of building the running totals beside the stones, you write them over the stones. It saves the room, and it takes something back that you might not notice.

The array you were handed is gone. You did not work on a copy of it. You worked on the caller's own row, and where the numbers used to be there are now totals. If anybody else was holding that row, they are holding something else now. This is the fourth time tonight has come round to it. The heading that must not change, the path that was saved while it was still moving, the one row wearing ten names. A name is not a thing. Here the sharing is deliberate and worth having, which is fine, so long as you know that you did it.

[chopper]"How would I ever know my answer was right?"[/] Chopper asked.

[robin]"Write the stupid one first."[/] Robin said. [robin]"The one that keeps the whole row and tries every move, exactly as the rules describe it. It is far too slow to hand in, and that is not what it is for. It is the thing you check the fast one against."[/]

Then make rows at random — short ones, two stones up to nine, a few hundred of them. Run both versions on every row. They have to agree every single time.

@read hush
This is worth doing even when you feel sure. The first two answers written for this problem got all three of the given examples right, and both of them were still wrong. Examples are not coverage. They were chosen to show you what the question means, and nobody ever promised they would find your mistake.

[robin]"And there is one signal worth learning to hear."[/] Robin said. [robin]"If you catch yourself writing and then they take this, and then we take that, inside your own recursion, stop. A clean one never has to say that. The minus sign was supposed to be handling the other player. If you are handling them by hand, it is the case that is wrong, and not the code."[/]

So the cost is one walk down the row and nothing else, which is O(n), and the room is one number. Robin did not reach for the chalk again. The wall stayed empty all the way to the end.

Robin said — [robin]"Remember only this much tonight. When a new problem arrives, first ask whether it is some old problem in new clothes."[/]

Robin put down the chalk. The table was full. Numbers sat quietly all across the wall, and each one carried the answer to its own small question.

She stepped back and looked at the whole table. There were hundreds of squares there, and a small answer in each. There was no magic in any one square, because the magic was in the order.

That is tonight's lesson. Large answers are built out of small answers, and small answers are built out of smaller answers still. Nobody has to work a miracle at any level, and all you have to do is get the order right.

Next comes the second lesson, which sets a lot of people free. The reason DP feels hard is not that the thinking is hard. The reason is that people forget to write down the case. Once you have written the one sentence saying which things together name your sub-question completely, the rest of the code arrives by itself.

Then there is the third lesson, which is the most practical. When a new question arrives, ask — [aside]"Which question that I already know is this the clothing of?"[/] There are few new questions in the world, and a great many new sets of clothes.
