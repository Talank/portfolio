So far the strip has been shapes. Tonight it becomes numbers, and there are only three of them, and every one of them is measured across the page, which means every one of them is a length of time.

Three measurements, three normal ranges, and each one, when it is abnormal, points at a different part of the road.

@read teach
The first is the PR interval.

It starts at the very beginning of the P wave and it ends at the very beginning of the QRS complex. Note that carefully — it starts where the atria start waking up and it ends where the ventricles start firing. So it is the whole journey, from the SA node, across the atria, through the AV node's deliberate wait, down the bundle and out to the muscle.

It should be between 0.12 and 0.20 seconds. In squares, which is how you will actually read it, that is three to five small squares.

Three to five. If you remember nothing else about the PR interval tonight, remember three to five.

[ne]पी आर तीनदेखि पाँच सानो घरसम्म हुनुपर्छ। यत्ति नै हो।[/]

@read teach
And what does it mean when it is wrong?

Longer than five small squares means the journey took too long, and almost always the delay is at the AV node, because that is the one place on the road that is allowed to be slow. A long PR interval is the first and gentlest of the heart blocks.

Shorter than three small squares means the current found a shortcut. Somewhere there is an extra strand of tissue bypassing the AV node, so the ventricles get the signal early, without waiting. That is pre-excitation, and it is a chapter of its own much later.

@read teach
The second measurement is the QRS duration, and it is the most useful number on the whole strip.

It is measured from the start of the complex to the end of it — from where the line first leaves the baseline to where it comes back and settles.

It should be under 0.12 seconds. Under three small squares. And three small squares is a distance your eye learns very quickly, because it is a little more than half of one big square.

@read teach
Here is why this number is worth more than the other two put together.

A narrow complex means the ventricles were lit up through the fast wiring. And the only way to reach the fast wiring is to come down through the AV node from above. So a narrow complex is a beat that was born above the ventricles. Every time. No exceptions worth worrying about tonight.

A wide complex means the signal did not use the wiring. Either it was born below the wiring, in the ventricular muscle itself, or it came from above and found the wiring broken. Either way, it had to crawl through muscle, and crawling is slow, and slow draws wide.

[sister]"Narrow is from upstairs. Wide is from downstairs, or the stairs are broken,"[/] Sister Maya says. [sister]"You will use that sentence more than any other."[/]

@read teach
The third measurement is the QT interval.

It runs from the very start of the QRS complex to the very end of the T wave, so it covers the whole of the ventricles firing and the whole of them resetting. It is, in effect, how long the ventricles are busy for.

And its normal value is not a single number, because it changes with the heart rate. A fast heart has a short QT interval and a slow heart has a long one, and both are entirely normal.

So there is a corrected value, written QTc, which is the QT interval adjusted for the rate, and the machine prints it at the top of every twelve-lead. Above about 0.44 seconds in a man and 0.46 in a woman is long, and a QTc above 0.50 is the number that makes cardiologists sit up.

@read teach
There is a bedside version of that which needs no arithmetic at all, and it is the one to actually carry.

At a normal rate, the QT interval should be less than half of the distance between two R waves. Look at the gap from one spike to the next spike, take half of it, and the T wave should have finished comfortably before that halfway mark.

If the T wave is still going at halfway, the QT is long, and a long QT is the setup for the single most dangerous rhythm in this whole course.

@algo naap
Three measurements, and every one of them is measured across the page. The PR interval runs from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS, and it should be three to five small squares. The QRS complex should be under three small squares wide, and anything at or beyond that is a wide complex, which means the beat did not use the heart's fast wiring. The QT interval runs from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave, and at a normal rate it should finish before halfway between two R waves. Three to five for the PR. Under three for the QRS. Under half for the QT.

One more thing before you sleep, and it is a habit rather than a fact.

Measure in the lead where the wave is widest, not where it is easiest. A QRS complex looks narrower in some leads than in others, because in some leads part of it happens to point straight across the electrode and draws almost nothing. If you measure in the flattering lead, you will call a wide complex narrow, and that is the exact mistake that gets somebody the wrong drug.

On a twelve-lead, look across all of them and measure the widest one. On a monitor, be honest that you might be looking at a flattering view, and be slower to call something narrow than to call it wide.

@tier medium

There is a fourth measurement people talk about, and it is deliberately not in tonight's three, and it is worth explaining why.

@read teach
The RR interval is the distance from one R wave to the next one. It is not really a measurement of the heart's electrics at all. It is a measurement of the rate and of the rhythm, and both of those get whole chapters shortly.

But it is the ruler that the QT interval is judged against, and it is the thing your eye actually uses when it decides whether a rhythm is regular, so it earns a name.

There is a companion to it called the PP interval, which is the distance from one P wave to the next. In a normal heart the PP interval and the RR interval are identical, because every P is followed by a QRS and nothing is being lost.

@read teach
And that gives you one of the most elegant tests in all of rhythm reading, which you will meet properly in three weeks.

When the PP intervals are regular and the RR intervals are regular but the two do not match each other, the atria and the ventricles have stopped talking. Each is keeping its own perfect time, and neither is listening. That is complete heart block, and you can see it without measuring anything except two rhythms that will not line up.

@tier long

The last thing tonight is about what the machine prints at the top of the page, and how much of it to trust.

@read teach
A modern twelve-lead machine prints a row of numbers above the tracing — the rate, the PR interval, the QRS duration, the QT and the QTc, and often an axis and an interpretation in capital letters.

The measurements are usually excellent. The machine measures across all twelve leads at once, finds the earliest onset and the latest offset of each complex, and it does not get tired at four in the morning. For the PR and the QRS in particular, the printed number is generally better than yours.

The interpretation is a different animal. It is a pattern matcher, it is tuned to be alarming rather than to be right, and it is wrong often enough that every hospital in the world requires a human to sign it.

@read teach
So the sensible habit is a split one. Read the numbers, and use them. Read the interpretation, and treat it as a colleague's guess — worth hearing, never worth signing.

And there is one specific trap. When the rhythm is irregular, the machine's rate is an average, and an average rate on an irregular rhythm can be a number the patient never actually had.
