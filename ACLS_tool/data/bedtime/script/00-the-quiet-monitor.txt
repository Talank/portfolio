# 0 — The Quiet Monitor

It is a little after two in the morning. The corridor lights are switched down to the night setting, which is not quite darkness and not quite light, and the floor gives back a long grey shine all the way to the far door.

There are six beds in this room. Five of them are asleep. Above the fourth bed a monitor is awake, and it is drawing a line.

There is a chair by the door. You can sit down in it. There is nothing you have to do right now.

The line comes out of the left side of the screen and travels to the right, and every second or so it lifts, stands up in a sharp spike, drops, and settles again. Then it does the same thing. Then it does the same thing again.

You have seen this a hundred times in a hundred rooms and never once looked at it. That is the honest place to start.

Nurse Maya is at the station with a cup of tea she has not touched. She has worked nights in this ward for eleven years. When she looks at that screen she does not see a wiggly line. She sees a sentence.

[maya]"People think reading an ECG is a talent,"[/] she says, without looking up. [maya]"It is not a talent. It is a habit. You look at the same five things in the same order, ten thousand times, and one day the answer arrives before you have finished looking."[/]

The other nurse on tonight is Laura. She is younger than Maya and she has already worked out the most useful thing about night shifts, which is that nobody learns anything after two in the morning by trying harder.

[laura]"You do not have to remember any of this tonight,"[/] she says, going past with an armful of folded blankets. [laura]"Just listen slowly. And sleep."[/]

Here is the thing nobody says out loud on the first day, and it is the thing that makes all the rest of it possible.

@read teach
A strip of ECG paper is not a picture of a heart. It is not an image, and it is not a scan. It is a graph, and it is the simplest graph there is[..]a pen, and a strip of paper being pulled underneath it.

Imagine that pen for a moment. It is held against the paper, and the paper slides along beneath it at a steady speed. If the pen never moved, you would get a straight flat line, all the way across, forever. That flat line is the whole ECG when nothing is happening.

Now the heart does something electrical. A wave of charge sweeps across it. The pen feels that and lifts, and then comes back down. And because the paper never stopped sliding, the mark it leaves is not a dot. It is a shape, spread out across the page.

That is all an ECG is. A pen that moves when the heart makes electricity, over paper that never stops moving.

@read teach
And once you have accepted that, you have already earned the two facts that everything else is built on.

The paper moves at a speed you know. Not a guess, not a setting somebody chose that night[..]a speed printed on the machine and agreed on by every hospital in the world. So the distance from one mark to another, measured across the page, is a length of time. Space across is time.

The pen moves up and down by an amount that follows the size of the voltage. So the height of a shape, measured up the page, is how big that electrical event was. Space up is size.

@read warm
Hold on to just those two things. Across the page is time. Up the page is size.

@algo kagaj
Here is the whole foundation, and it is two sentences long. The paper moves sideways at a speed you know, so distance across the page is time. The pen moves up and down with the voltage the heart makes, so height on the page is size. Time across. Size up. Every rule you will ever learn about an ECG is measured in one of those two directions, and nothing else.

@read teach
Now, why does any of this matter tonight, at two in the morning, when nothing is wrong with anybody?

Because in six months, or in a year, something will be wrong with somebody, and it will be wrong quickly. And the difference between the people who help in that room and the people who stand at the back is almost never courage. It is that some of them can look at the monitor and say a word out loud, and the rest cannot.

The word is worth a lot. Say the right word and the room knows which algorithm it is in, which drug is drawn up, whether a defibrillator is charged or left alone. Say nothing, and the room waits.

Being able to say that one word out loud is what all of this is for.

So this is what these nights are for. Not the drugs, not the doses, not the flowcharts[..]the tool on your phone already drills those, and drills them better than a story can. These nights are for the one skill the flowcharts all quietly assume you already have. Reading the line.

There is one more thing worth knowing before the lights go down, and it is a warning rather than a fact.

@read teach
The screen above bed four and the paper strip in your hand are not the same object, and people confuse them constantly.

The screen is a monitor. It is watching one or two views of the heart, continuously, and it is doing it through sticky electrodes that were put on in a hurry and have been there since yesterday. It is very good at one question: what is the rhythm right now. It is unreliable at almost every other question, because it filters heavily to keep the picture steady and because the electrodes move every time the patient breathes.

The paper is a recording. It is usually a twelve-lead recording, taken deliberately, with the patient lying still, from twelve different angles at once. It is very good at the questions the monitor cannot answer: which part of the muscle is injured, how wide a complex truly is, whether that hump is real.

@read teach
So the sentence to carry is this one. Rhythm you may read from the monitor. Injury you must read from the twelve-lead.

Nurse Maya has a shorter version of it, which she says to every new person in the ward, usually once and never again.

[maya]"The monitor tells you what the heart is doing. The paper tells you what has happened to it."[/]

@tier long

The last thing tonight is the smallest, and it is about a letter.

@read teach
You will see this test called an ECG and you will also see it called an EKG, and both are correct, and nobody is being clever.

The machine was invented by a Dutch physiologist, and the German spelling of the middle word begins with a K rather than a C. American hospitals largely kept the German spelling, and the rest of the world largely did not. That is the entire story. There is no difference in the test, the paper, the leads or the reading.

There is one small practical reason the German spelling survived in busy places, and it is a good one. Spoken in a loud room, the letters E C G and the letters E E G sound almost identical, and an E E G is a recording of the brain rather than the heart. E K G does not collide with anything.

@read warm
Both names mean the same thing. There is no difference at all between them.
