Now let us get to know three shapes at once.

A set is a collection with no duplicates. It answers the question of whether some thing is there or not at once.

A queue is a line. Things are added at the back, and taken from the front.

A deque is a line with both ends open. Adding at the front is allowed, and adding at the back is allowed.

And there is a fourth as well, and that is the priority queue. Here the order of the line is settled not by the time of coming, but by importance.

A hospital's emergency room is like this. Not whoever came first, but whoever is most serious goes first.

Now listen to today's rule.

@algo teen-bhanda
There are four vessels, and which to use is settled by your question. If your question is "was this thing met before or not" use a set, and no duplicate ever sits there. If your question is "let the first to come go first" use a queue, and in Java an ArrayDeque is the best thing for that. If you have to add and take away at both ends use a deque, and that does the work of a stack as well. If your question is "which is the smallest or the largest right now" use a priority queue.

This is true. Inside a HashSet there is a HashMap, and there every key is there and the value is empty.

This is why all the rules of the island before apply here as well. Write equals and hash code together, and keep the members immutable.

Here is how to check that you picked the right vessel, without writing any code. Say your question in one sentence, and listen for its first word. "Have I seen" wants a set. "Who is next" wants a queue. "Which is worst" wants a priority queue. If the sentence begins "what is at position", you did not want any of these, and you wanted a list.

Now I will say an old piece of advice, which has changed these days.

Old books say to use the class called Stack for a stack. But that class is very old, and it puts a lock on at every step.

These days an ArrayDeque is used for a stack too. Put in from the top, and take out from the top.

The same thing serves for a queue as well. This is why the ArrayDeque is among the most useful collections in Java.

Now let us get to know the set's brothers. A HashSet has no order. A LinkedHashSet keeps the order things were put in. A TreeSet keeps them in order from small to large.

And let me say a small and sweet use. If you have a list, and duplicates have to be taken out of it, put it into a HashSet, and bring it back into a list.

But be careful. Doing this the order is lost. If the order has to be saved as well use a LinkedHashSet, and that does both pieces of work.

At night Sanji made the kitchen light dim, and sat on the stool.

[aside]"I feel like telling you something,"[/] he said. [aside]"In a kitchen every pot has one piece of work."[/]

"The pan is large, but tea is not made in it. The kettle is small, but rice is not cooked in it. Knowing how to choose a pot is half of cooking."

The same holds in collections. A list can do every piece of work, but a list should not do every piece of work.

Let me give an example. Suppose you have ten thousand names, and you have to check for every new name whether it came before.

If you use a list, the whole list has to be read for every check. For ten thousand names that is five crore comparisons.

If you use a set, every check ends in one step. For ten thousand checks only ten thousand steps are taken.

The difference in the code is one word. The difference in the time is thousands of times over.

Now listen to one use of the priority queue, which will be of service in your log pose.

When searching, hundreds of notes may agree. But you want only the ten that agree best.

Putting them all in order is expensive. Keep instead a priority queue of ten, and when something better is found throw away the weakest of them.
